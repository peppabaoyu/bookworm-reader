The Lantern on the Hill
by A. Reader

Chapter One: The Long Walk

The road out of the village was narrow and old, and in the late afternoon the light lay along it like honey poured over stone. Thomas walked with his coat collar turned up, because the wind had turned cold, and he counted the milestone markers as he passed them, out of a habit begun in childhood and never abandoned.

At the fourth marker he stopped to rest. The hill above him was dark with pines, and somewhere near the top a small light was burning, steady and yellow, like a lamp held behind a curtain. He had seen that light every evening for a week, and every evening he had told himself the same thing: tomorrow I will climb up and ask who keeps it.

The river ran below the road, and the sound of the water was the only sound in the world. He thought about the letter in his pocket, which he had read so many times that he knew the fold lines by heart. The letter said very little, but what it said was enough to bring a man a hundred miles to a village whose name he had never heard before this month.

He resumed his walk. The miles passed under his boots with the patience of miles that have nothing better to do. When the last bend of the road revealed the inn, its windows were warm, and the smell of woodsmoke mixed with the smell of wet leaves, and he felt, for the first time in a long while, that arriving was a pleasure and not merely the end of going.

Chapter Two: The Keeper of the Light

In the morning the innkeeper would tell him nothing at all about the light on the hill. "Ask the schoolteacher," the man said, wiping the table with a cloth that had seen better decades. "The schoolteacher knows everything, and is glad to prove it."

The schoolteacher was a spare woman with quick grey eyes, and she did know everything, or nearly. The lamp on the hill, she explained, had been kept burning for thirty years by a man named Aldous, who had come to the village as a young engineer to build a bridge, and had remained after the bridge was finished for reasons that nobody remembered clearly and nobody dared to invent.

"Every evening, without fail," she said. "In fog, in rain, in the two weeks the fever came through. Some say he is signaling to someone across the valley. Some say he is keeping a promise. He himself says only that a light which goes out is very hard to relight, and that this is the whole of the matter."

Thomas thanked her and walked out into the bright cold of the morning. The hill stood against the sky, patient as an arithmetic problem, and he made up his mind that this would be the evening. He would climb, and knock, and ask.

Chapter Three: What the Lamp Said

The climb took an hour. The pines were close and quiet, and the needle-covered path switched back and forth, and twice he surprised a bird that scolded him roundly for existing. When at last he came out of the trees, the small stone house stood on the bald top of the hill, and the lamp was in its window, burning exactly as it had burned every evening of the past week.

The door opened before he knocked.

"I wondered when you would come up," said the old man, who was smaller and more ordinary than Thomas had imagined. "Sit down. Mind the wires. Tea will be ready in four minutes."

Inside there were tools, and coils of copper wire, and a great many notebooks, and one wall covered with careful maps of the valley. Thomas looked at the maps and understood nothing, and said so.

"Good," said the keeper, pouring the tea. "The maps are not the point. The point is the light. Sit by it. Watch it. Tell me on Thursday what you have seen."

And that is how Thomas came to spend a winter on the hill, trimming wicks and cleaning glass and learning, slowly, what a light is for: not to show the way home, but to say, in a language older than words, that somebody is awake.
